# Go / No-Go Memo — DACH Market Entry

## Context
The organisation had decided to enter the DACH market as its first European expansion.

Initial assumptions around market demand, product relevance, and timing were positive.
Internal alignment existed at board level, and a preliminary budget had been earmarked.

However, no operational market presence existed.
Customer feedback was indirect.
Decision-making remained largely centralised outside the region.

## Decision at Hand
The decision was whether to proceed with a full DACH market entry
or to pause further commitments until operational reality could be validated.

This decision had to be made before structural and financial commitments
would materially limit optionality.

## Irreversible Commitments
Proceeding would have required several irreversible commitments:

- Legal entity setup
- Senior local leadership hire
- Long-term budget allocation
- Formal go-to-market signalling towards customers and partners

Once initiated, these steps would have been difficult to reverse
without reputational and organisational cost.

## Options Considered
Three options were considered:

1. Proceed with full market entry as planned
2. Delay the decision and continue preparatory activities
3. Pause market entry and revalidate under real market conditions

## Decision
The decision was to pause the DACH market entry
and not proceed with irreversible commitments at this stage.

## Rationale
The available signals did not justify irreversible commitments.

Market feedback remained indirect.
Operational assumptions had not been tested under real conditions.
Decision ownership was still partially diffused across headquarters functions.

Proceeding would have converted uncertainty into structural cost
rather than into learning.

## Consequences
Pausing the market entry carried immediate consequences:

- Short-term disappointment at board and management level
- Loss of perceived momentum
- Internal pressure to justify the pause

However, it preserved optionality
and avoided committing the organisation to a structure
that had not yet proven viable.

## What Would Have Broken Otherwise
Proceeding would have created the appearance of progress
while reducing the organisation’s ability to correct course.

A local structure would have been built
before product-market reality was understood.
Leadership would have been hired
into an environment lacking true decision authority.
Stopping later would have been politically harder
and organisationally more expensive.

The pause prevented a silent failure.
